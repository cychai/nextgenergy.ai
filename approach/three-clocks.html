Approach · 03

A leak is one word hiding three different failures.

Liquid leaks have entered compute-availability management: NVIDIA Mission Control surfaces a detected leak as a compute-node state alongside node health, not only as a facilities alarm. Most project boundaries have not caught up. They still compress three risks into one checkbox: leak detection installed.

Clock one

Manufacturing

Owner: the factory

What it looks like. A weld or braze that escapes the factory. A void in a cold-plate seam. A crimp outside tolerance.

What survives as evidence. Bounded factory acceptance test (FAT) leak-test records, traceable to serial number. Crimp process parameters. Material certificates for elastomers and wetted metals.

Clock two

Commissioning

Owner: the integrator and the commissioning agent

What it looks like. A quick disconnect disturbed during install. A field weld that was flushed but never passivated. A fitting that looked dry at handover.

What survives as evidence. Connection records for every joint made or disturbed on site. Flushing and cleanliness results. Pressure tests against written acceptance criteria. Tested compute-and-liquid isolation sequences.

Clock three

Service life

Owner: the operator

What it looks like. A hose that ages out in year four. An elastomer that hardened. A fluid whose inhibitor package was never topped up.

What survives as evidence. Fluid-quality trends. System-performance trends against commissioning baselines. Service logs for every disturbance of a boundary. Replacement intervals with a basis.

A sensor tells you liquid has arrived. It cannot tell you who created the risk.

Leak detection is necessary and it is late. By the time a rope sensor is wet, the question is no longer whether there is a leak but whether the response sequence works, whether the compute can be isolated from the liquid safely, and what evidence survives the incident. None of that is answered by the sensor. It is answered by a chain that was built before the first drop:

  • bounded FAT leak-test records
  • connection and flushing records
  • fluid-quality and system-performance trends
  • tested compute-and-liquid isolation sequences

What gets documented can be priced

Project owners, and increasingly their insurers and lenders, may ask which clock a project has covered. You can price evidence. You cannot price "the work was done carefully". A project that runs well for years and leaves no records has succeeded operationally and failed commercially the first time someone asks for proof.

Reference: NVIDIA Mission Control Systems Administration Guide, leak detection.

We build the three clocks into the scope. The factory clock is covered by what we require of suppliers before a component is released. The commissioning clock is our own work and is recorded joint by joint. The service clock is handed to the operator as trended baselines, not as a binder.
