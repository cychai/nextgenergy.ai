Evidence · Reference

Ten leak points every liquid-cooling team should know.

Not a ranking. A checklist, following the coolant from chip to facility. For each of the ten, a turnover package should answer: was it tested, against what acceptance criteria, and where is the record.

Grouped by position on the path
  • At the server
  • In the rack
  • In the CDU
  • Between the CDU and the facility

First published by Jim Li on LinkedIn, September 2026. Kept here as a stable reference. The wording is deliberately vendor-neutral: it names practices, not people or products.

Score your last project

At the server

  1. Cold plate joints

    Brazed seams, friction-stir welds, covers and ports. Voids, incomplete bonding and microcracks sit upstream of the smallest flow passages.

  2. Quick disconnects

    Often the highest-count removable connection in the rack. A disconnected QD is not automatically a sealed one. One particle on the poppet seat may be enough.

    → First-fill cleanliness is leak prevention, not housekeeping.

  3. Hose crimps

    Crimp dimensions, concentricity, hose variation and material relaxation all matter. Once the sleeve is on, most defects are invisible.

    → A crimp is a controlled manufacturing process, not an assembly step.

In the rack

  1. Manifold branches

    Every server connection adds another boundary, and every server replacement disturbs it again.

  2. Elastomeric seals

    They sit inside QDs, valves, adapters and manifolds. Compression set, hardening, temperature and fluid incompatibility can turn a dry joint into a future leak.

    → "Compatible with water" is not a material specification.

  3. Threaded, compression and adapter fittings

    Preparation, alignment, support and torque all matter.

    → Looking dry at commissioning is not evidence of how a fitting was assembled or tested.

In the CDU

  1. Internal boundaries

    Pump seals, heat-exchanger gaskets, valve stems, filter housings and service covers. All enclosed, so a small leak may remain hidden until it reaches the cabinet floor or a sensor.

Between the CDU and the facility

  1. Field welds and hot work

    A weld is both a pressure boundary and a water-chemistry event.

    → Flushing removes transportable debris. It does not, by itself, passivate.

  2. Balancing and isolation valves

    Bodies, stems, unions and serviceable closures. Several boundaries at every location, operated during commissioning and then largely left alone.

  3. Fill, drain, vent and sample ports

    Small connections, frequently disturbed: temporary hoses connected and removed, caps replaced, valves reopened.

The point is not that factory joints never leak. It is that a boundary made under controlled conditions can be tested, recorded and released against defined acceptance criteria. A boundary made in a live hall at 2 a.m. deserves exactly the same evidence.

On your last liquid-cooling project, how many of the ten could you prove, not just say were checked?
